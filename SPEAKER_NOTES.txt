DARRSHENI  ·  20 sec

Good morning. We are Darrsheni, Yuvraj and Nishkarsh.

Our project builds on a CVPR 2025 paper called WPFormer. It finds cracks on
surfaces - not "there is a crack somewhere", but exactly which pixels.

We reproduced it, then tested five common tricks people use to improve such
models. I'll cover the paper, Yuvraj shows a video, Nishkarsh explains what we
built, Yuvraj gives the numbers.

Fair warning - most of our results are negative. That turned out to be the
interesting part.

=============================================================
SLIDE 2
=============================================================
DARRSHENI  ·  55 sec

Look at the picture. There is a crack running across it. Take a second - it is
not easy to see. That is the point.

Three things make this hard.

Cracks are THIN - sometimes three pixels wide. Shrink the image once and the
crack vanishes.

They are INVISIBLE - grey crack on grey concrete, almost no difference in
brightness.

And they are RARE. Only about one and a half percent of pixels are crack.

So think what that means. A useless model that just says "no crack" everywhere
is still 98.6 percent correct.

That one fact explains almost everything in this project.

=============================================================
SLIDE 3
=============================================================
DARRSHENI  ·  40 sec

So how was this solved before?

A network reads the image, and at the very end one convolution layer asks each
pixel - crack, or not?

The problem is that this last layer is the same for every image. Once training
ends, its numbers never change.

Think of a rubber stamp. You press the same stamp on every photo forever.
Bright scratch on steel? Same stamp. Faint crack on concrete? Same stamp.

The paper calls it a single image-independent query. In plain words - it cannot
adapt to what it is looking at.

=============================================================
SLIDE 4
=============================================================
DARRSHENI  ·  50 sec

WPFormer replaces that one frozen stamp with sixteen learnable queries.

Think of sixteen detectives. Each one looks at THIS image and forms its own
opinion of where the crack is. They are not frozen - they react to the photo in
front of them.

Then each proposes a mask and says how confident it is, and the final answer is
a weighted blend of all sixteen.

In the diagram, PVTv2 reads the image at four sizes, the FPN combines them, and
the queries pass through three decoder blocks.

Inside each block are the paper's two real ideas - WCA and PCA. That is the next
slide.

=============================================================
SLIDE 5
=============================================================
DARRSHENI  ·  55 sec

Two ideas.

WCA, the wavelet idea. Any image splits into blurry shapes and sharp detail -
bass and treble, like music. A crack is a thin sharp line, so it lives in the
treble. Turn the treble up and faint cracks appear.

The catch is that treble also carries hiss. Crack edges and camera noise sit
together. So the model learns a volume knob - noise down, crack untouched.

PCA, the prototype idea. There are about 2,300 positions to look at, and nearly
all are boring background that drowns out the crack. So squeeze them into
sixteen summary cards.

Why not just delete the background? Other methods do. But if their guess is
wrong, the missed part is gone forever. Summarising keeps every pixel.

=============================================================
SLIDE 6
=============================================================
DARRSHENI  ·  30 sec

Against seventeen other methods, WPFormer wins on every measure.

The number to remember all talk is wF-beta. It balances - did you find the
crack, and did you also mark things that were not cracks. Higher is better. The
paper gets 0.7672.

One thing before I hand over. These scores are only around 0.75. On ordinary
photos these same networks score far higher. So this problem is not solved - and
that is why we thought there was room to improve it.

Over to Yuvraj.

SLIDE 8
=============================================================
NISHKARSH  ·  50 sec

Before what we changed, how we made the test fair. This matters more than it
sounds.

First - we compare against our OWN baseline, not the paper's printed number.
Different GPU, different libraries. Comparing to their number would compare two
setups, not two methods.

Second - we froze the data split. CrackSeg9k has no validation set, so we cut
ten percent out of training once and saved the exact file names. Every
experiment uses that same split.

Third - every run gets exactly thirty epochs. Same time for everyone.

And why so strict? If you pick your best model by looking at the test set, you
have already used it. It is not a test any more.

=============================================================
SLIDE 9
=============================================================
NISHKARSH  ·  55 sec

Five things we tried. None are random - the literature says all five work well.

Bigger backbone - simply more capacity.

Structure loss - right now every pixel counts the same, so 98.6 percent
background drowns everything. This makes crack pixels count five times more.

Stronger augmentation - flip, rotate, change colours. A crack has no correct
direction, so an upside-down crack is still a valid crack.

EMA - instead of the weights from the last training step, average the last few
hundred steps.

Test-time augmentation - show the model twelve views of the same photo and
average. No retraining at all.

Now the key design choice. Every one is a switch, OFF by default. So with
nothing on, our code reproduces the paper exactly. Each experiment differs by
exactly ONE thing. If a number moves, we know what moved it.

=============================================================
SLIDE 10
=============================================================
NISHKARSH  ·  55 sec

Two things we only found by reading the authors' code line by line.

First - our plan set aside several DAYS to swap the backbone. We assumed we'd
rewrite all the connecting layers.

Then we looked. Every version of PVTv2 has exactly the same channel widths - 64,
128, 320, 512, identical. The bigger ones are deeper, not wider. So nothing else
needed changing. Days of planned work became one line.

Second, and this one is funny. The data loader computes an edge map for every
image on every batch - thousands of times during training. And the training file
never reads it. Computed, then thrown straight in the bin.

That edge map is exactly the boundary signal we wanted. Already there, already
running, nobody using it.

Lesson - read the code, not just the paper. Over to Yuvraj.

SLIDE 11
=============================================================
YUVRAJ  ·  80 sec  -  SLOW DOWN, this is the main slide

Right. We ran all five. Here is what happened.

Our baseline scored 0.7390. That is what everything has to beat.

Second row, green - bigger backbone. 0.7511. Plus 1.64 percent, and the only
change that improved every metric at once. That one worked.

Now look at the rest. All red.

EMA - slightly worse, but that is really just noise.

Structure loss - worse by one percent. And this was the change we were most
confident about.

Add the reweighting on top - worse again.

Last row. Stronger augmentation. Minus 3.78 percent. The biggest drop, and the
one that shocked us, because augmentation is supposed to be nearly free.

So four out of five made the model WORSE.

[PAUSE - let it land]

The obvious question is why. That is the interesting part.

=============================================================
SLIDE 12
=============================================================
YUVRAJ  ·  55 sec

Here is the experiment that gave us the answer.

Test-time augmentation needs no training. So we took one finished model, kept it
completely fixed, and only changed how many views we averaged.

Plain image - 0.7511. Add a left-right flip - basically the same. Add an
upside-down flip - drops to 0.7189. Add zooming - drops again to 0.7024.

Now the middle column - views the model never saw during training. Zero, zero,
two, ten.

See it? The more unfamiliar views we mixed in, the worse it got. Perfectly in
order.

And here is why. The authors only ever flipped images left to right during
training. Never upside down. Never zoomed. So an upside-down crack means nothing
to this model.

Same weights, nothing retrained. Only the views changed.

=============================================================
SLIDE 13
=============================================================
YUVRAJ  ·  55 sec

So why did four of five fail?

Look at the curves. Not one line has flattened out - they are all still climbing
when training stops.

And the box on the right - best epoch for each run: 27, 29, 30, 28, 28. Out of
30. Every run peaked right at the end.

In plain words - none of them had finished learning. We ran out of time, not out
of improvement.

Now, three of our four failed ideas make the training job HARDER. That is a
trade - you pay the cost immediately and collect the reward much later, once the
model settles.

We stopped at thirty epochs. Nothing had settled. So we paid and never
collected.

And the one that worked - the bigger backbone - does not make the job harder. It
just adds capacity, which pays off straight away.

To be honest, this is our best explanation, not something we proved.

=============================================================
SLIDE 14
=============================================================
YUVRAJ  ·  40 sec

So where did we land.

We aimed for two to three percent. We got 1.64. We did not reach our target, and
we are telling you that instead of moving the goalposts.

But I don't think that makes this a failed project.

We have a clean negative result - four popular techniques, tested properly, one
variable at a time, all failing on this problem. That saves the next person the
time we spent.

We have a mechanism, not a shrug - the TTA experiment shows why, on a fixed
model.

And we know the next experiment: train twice as long. If we are right, some
failures should turn into wins. We ran out of GPU budget.

If we had reported plus two percent and hidden four failures, this would be a
worse project.

ALL THREE  ·  questions  (not spoken - reference only)

"Why only 30 epochs?" - GPU budget, about 21 GPU-hours on Colab. The key point
is every run got the SAME 30, so the comparison is fair.

"Is 1.64% significant?" - One seed, so no error bars. But the effects we
interpret are 0.012 to 0.028 - ten times bigger than the 0.0026 we call noise.

"Why is your baseline below the paper's 0.7672?" - Two of our own choices. Half
the epochs, 30 not 60. And our frozen split removes 724 images, so we train on
about 10 percent less data. We accepted that because picking models on the test
set would invalidate everything.

"Why not Focal Tversky?" - It has three parameters to tune, structure loss has
one. No compute for the search.

"What next?" - The 60-epoch pair, to test the under-training explanation.

"What is that purple line stopping early in the curves?" - The combined run. We
abandoned it once we saw the individual components fail.
